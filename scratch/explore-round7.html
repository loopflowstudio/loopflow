Six backlogs

Which of these worlds should choosing the next work live in? Each one answers “what do I do next?” with a different mechanism, not a different layout.

Rounds 1 to 6 settled how a backlog should read: a library of compact rows beside a full brief. That page is kept at explore-round6.html. This round asks a question those rounds did not: with 46 unstarted Product Tasks, what makes five of them stand out?

Every sketch runs on the real 46 unstarted Product Tasks from the November 14 snapshot. Nothing here writes anywhere; reload resets it. exists marks what Loopflow has today, new what the vision adds.

1 · The line

A Project's order has one line in it. The few Tasks above it are what you would start next; the pile below is only ever searched.

Product · chapter orderLine holds

Try “Above the line” on something in the pile: the last Task above drops below. Search the pile for “Rex”.

DemoOpen Product in Desktop. Five Tasks with their briefs' openings fill the screen, and the same five sit under the Wave in the sidebar. lf task list --next prints them. Pull LOO-798 above the line; LOO-423 drops into the pile.
AppealChoosing becomes a small decision made often. The 41 you are not doing stop asking to be read.
CostSmall. Rank per Project exists (lf task update --rank). The line is one number per Project new, plus the split view and sidebar shelf.
RiskThe pile rots unseen. Nothing here tells you a pile item became urgent or obsolete.

2 · Loose ends

Most of this backlog was left behind by finished work. Grouped by where each Task came from, 46 items become 13 threads you can close.

The grouping is real: it comes from the first Task each brief says it follows. 26 of the 46 name one. Origin titles are read from the store today.

DemoOpen Product. “Task conversations are ready in Desktop without clicking Open” (LOO-672, done) shows four loose ends. Open the thread, read the four briefs together, start the two that matter, abandon one with a reason. The thread shows 1 left.
AppealYou remember the work that spawned a follow-up far better than the follow-up. Closing a thread feels like finishing something; ranking 46 rows never does.
CostMedium. Origin is prose in the brief today; a recorded “follows” link from Task to Task is new, in the planning writer and in Linear sync. Capture from a Task conversation would set it.
Risk20 Tasks have no origin and land in one big group. Threads say where work came from, not whether it is worth doing.

3 · The plan is a file

The backlog opens as text. You move lines, put a verb in front of one, save, and read exactly what will change before it does.

lf plan edit --wave product

Try: cut a line and paste it higher; type drop before one; type start before another; add a line with no ID. Then Save.

Demolf plan edit opens the Project in $EDITOR. Move six lines, mark two drop, write one new idea, save. lf prints the eleven changes and asks once. Desktop shows the same file in a pane with the same preview. An agent in the Wave conversation proposes an edited file and you read its diff.
AppealBulk reordering at typing speed. One surface for people, CLI and agents, and every batch change has a preview for free.
CostSmall to medium. Update, rank, move and abandon exist per Task. The file format, its parser and the batch apply with per-line results are new, in Rust.
RiskTitles only: a line does not show its brief. A stale file saved after someone else reordered needs a clear answer per line.

4 · What the chapter needs

The chapter's KRs are the headings. A Task either sits under the KR it moves or waits outside the chapter, so “why this next?” is on screen.

Product · Chapter demo-20260924

Both KRs are the chapter's real ones. The starting placements are my illustration; move any Task with its menu.

DemoOpen Product. Two KRs head the page. “Three two-hour Desktop sessions without a crash” has four Tasks under it; place LOO-831 there from the unplaced pile. Starting a Task shows which KR it serves in its conversation header.
AppealTies backlog to the Wave's objective, which nothing does today. An empty KR and a long unplaced pile are both things you can act on.
CostMedium. KRs per Project exist. A link from Task to KR is new; I found none in the store. Overlaps LOO-514, which owns how Wave views fit together.
RiskOnly as good as the KRs. Two KRs about Desktop stability make most of this backlog “unplaced”, which may be true or may just be thin KRs.

5 · Tonight's run

The backlog has two speeds. Small, well-specified work is marked safe to run unattended and leaves in batches; what remains is short enough to choose from by hand.

Stand-in for a person's mark: every Task whose brief is under 1,100 characters starts in the unattended list. Tick a few and send.

DemoOpen Product at six. Sixteen Tasks are marked unattended. Tick five, press Send, read the list of what will happen, confirm. They appear under Autonomous. In the morning, three are merged and two wait with one question each.
AppealUses what Loopflow has that a tracker does not: the pile can shrink without you. “Say ‘builtin default’ instead of ‘default default’” should never need choosing.
CostMedium. Running to merge with Auto-Ship exists (lf -b task run), as does the Autonomous section. The unattended mark, a batch launch with per-Task results and a morning summary are new.
RiskFive merges you did not watch. A vague brief run unattended produces a confident wrong PR. Says nothing about choosing among the big ones.

6 · The backlog that prepares itself

Radical success. An agent reads every unstarted Task against the code as it is now, and the backlog opens as a short list of decisions with their reasons attached.

checked against main, last night

These findings are invented to show the shape. No agent has checked any of these Tasks.

DemoOpen Product on Monday. “8 decisions” replaces “Backlog 46”. Two Tasks look already fixed, each citing the commit; close them in one click each. Two pairs overlap; read both briefs side by side and keep one. Two have a single question whose answer makes them startable. The other 34 are untouched and one click away.
AppealThe work of grooming, which nobody does, is done. Every backlog Task you open has been read against today's code.
CostLarge. A scheduled Wave pass, a proposal record that cites its source and never writes on its own, review UI, and agent spend on 46 Tasks per pass, all new. Abandon, update and comment exist for applying an accepted proposal.
RiskA convincing wrong “already fixed” closes real work. Proposals pile up into a second backlog if they arrive faster than you decide.

How they sit together

1 and 3 only use rank, which exists; they are the two that could ship without a new planning concept. 2 and 4 each need one new link on a Task (to its origin, or to a KR) and answer different questions: where did this come from, and what is it for. 5 and 6 are the two where agents act on the backlog; 5 on your mark, 6 on a schedule.

None of them replaces reading a brief. Each could hand a selected Task to the round 6 reader.
